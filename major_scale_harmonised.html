1. The major scale

Seven notes built from a pattern of whole (W) and half (H) steps: W W H W W W H. On the guitar a whole step is two frets, a half step is one. In C that gives C D E F G A B, the white notes of a piano.

2. Harmonising means stacking thirds

To harmonise a scale, build a chord on every note using only notes from that scale. Take a note, skip one, take the next, skip one, take the next. On C: C E G. On D: D F A. On E: E G B. Each chord is named after its starting note, its root.

3. Why the qualities change

A major third is 4 frets, a minor third is 3 frets. Because the scale has uneven steps, the stacked thirds come out different:

DegreeThirdsQualityIn C
I, IV, V4 + 3majorC, F, G
ii, iii, vi3 + 4minorDm, Em, Am
vii°3 + 3diminishedBdim

The pattern is the same in every key: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished. Learn it once and you know all twelve keys.

4. Roman numerals

Upper case is major, lower case is minor. They describe the chord's job in any key. I–IV–V in G is G–C–D; in A it is A–D–E. This is why songwriters and bands talk in numbers.

5. Try it

Choose G in the key menu. Those chords (G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em, F#dim) are the whole open-chord family of G. Strum I, IV, V and then vi. Switch to C and notice that F and G sit next to your Am and Em shapes.

1. Seventh chords

Add one more third on top and you get four-note chords: Imaj7, ii7, iii7, IVmaj7, V7, vi7, viiø7. Press the Triads/7ths button above to switch. Only V gets the dominant 7th, a major triad with a minor seventh, and that is what makes it pull so hard toward home.

4. Nashville numbers and capo

Because the pattern is the same everywhere, you can play a song in G with a capo on fret 2 and the shapes stay the same while the key becomes A. Learn progressions as numbers and you can transpose instantly.

5. The 12-bar blues

I7 I7 I7 I7 / IV7 IV7 I7 I7 / V7 IV7 I7 V7. It borrows the V7 sound for all three chords, a deliberate step outside the strict harmonised scale.

1. Modes are the same notes with a new home

Each chord implies a scale built from the same seven notes starting on its root: I Ionian, ii Dorian, iii Phrygian, IV Lydian, V Mixolydian, vi Aeolian, vii Locrian. Over Dm7 in C, play D Dorian (D E F G A B C). You are not changing notes, you are changing which note feels like home. Tap a chord above to see its scale on the neck, with the chord tones highlighted.

2. Avoid notes

Over a major chord the natural 4th clashes with the 3rd (F over C). Play it as a passing tone or use IV's Lydian #4 instead. Over iii (Phrygian) the b2 is the characteristic colour and is quite dark; over vii it is the b5 that gives Locrian its instability.

3. Extensions

Keep stacking thirds: 9th, 11th, 13th (the same notes an octave up). Diatonic 9ths work on every degree. The natural 11th is only safe on the minor chords (ii, iii, vi); on IV use the #11 and on I and V leave it out. The 13th works well on V.

4. Guide-tone voice leading

In ii–V–I, track the 3rds and 7ths. In C: Dm7 (F, C) → G7 (B, F) → Cmaj7 (E, B). The 7th of each chord falls by a half step or step to the 3rd of the next: C→B, F→E. Voicing with just these two notes plus the root outlines the whole progression and is the basis of jazz comping.

5. Going outside the scale

Secondary dominants: a V7 of any diatonic chord. In C: A7→Dm (V7/ii), E7→Am (V7/vi), D7→G (V7/V), B7→Em (V7/iii), C7→F (V7/IV).
Modal interchange: borrow chords from the parallel minor: bVII (Bb), bVI (Ab), bIII (Eb), iv (Fm). The Beatles-style IV–iv–I is a classic example.
Tritone substitution: replace V7 with the dominant a tritone away (Db7 for G7). It shares the same 3rd and 7th, with the roles swapped.

6. On the guitar

Use the triad viewer above. Play I, IV and V as triads on strings 4-3-2, then 3-2-1, then 5-4-3, using one position at a time. Watch how little your hand moves. Then add the 7ths and see which note changes between neighbouring chords. This is how you stop playing shapes and start playing harmony.

Pitch, octave and the semitone

Pitch is how high or low a note sounds, set by the frequency of vibration. Double the frequency and you hear the same note an octave higher, so notes are named by letter and repeat every octave. Western music divides the octave into 12 equal steps called semitones (half steps). On guitar one fret is one semitone. A tone (whole step) is two semitones. The 12 notes are C C#/Db D D#/Eb E F F#/Gb G G#/Ab A A#/Bb B. The same pitch with two names (F# and Gb) is called an enharmonic pair.

The natural notes have uneven gaps: there is no black key between E and F or between B and C, so those pairs are a semitone apart and the rest are a tone.

An interval is the distance between two notes. Its number counts letter names (C to E is a 3rd because C D E), and its quality comes from the semitone count.

Perfect intervals (unison, 4th, 5th, octave) have one standard size. 2nds, 3rds, 6ths and 7ths come in major and minor forms, a semitone apart. Shrink a minor or perfect interval by a semitone and it becomes diminished; widen a major or perfect one and it becomes augmented.

Why a seven-note scale

A scale is a chosen subset of the 12 notes. The major scale is a diatonic scale: seven notes, each letter name used once, with five whole steps and two half steps. The half steps sit between degrees 3 and 4 and between 7 and 8, which is what gives the scale its shape.

It can be read as two identical four-note groups (tetrachords), W W H, joined by a whole step: C D E F + G A B C. The same pattern starting on G gives G A B C + D E F# G, which is why moving the key up a fifth adds one sharp.

The same seven notes also come from stacking perfect fifths. Starting on F: F C G D A E B. Six fifths yield the seven notes, and folding them into one octave gives the scale. This is why the fifth is the most important interval in tonal music after the octave.

The scale degrees (relative to the tonic) are the major 2nd, major 3rd, perfect 4th, perfect 5th, major 6th and major 7th. Any scale with exactly these intervals is major, in any key.

Where the intervals come from

Strike a string and it vibrates in whole and fractional parts at once, giving the harmonic series: partials at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 times the base frequency. The ratios between neighbours are the simple intervals of tonal music: 2:1 octave, 3:2 perfect fifth, 4:3 perfect fourth, 5:4 major third, 6:5 minor third. Simple ratios sound consonant, complex ones sound tense.

Modern instruments use equal temperament, where every semitone is the same ratio (the 12th root of 2). This lets one instrument play in every key. The fifth is almost pure (700 cents against 702), but the equal-tempered major third is about 14 cents wider than the pure 5:4, which is one reason a fretted guitar chord is never perfectly in tune.

Building chords by stacking thirds

Western harmony is tertian: chords are built from stacked thirds. Each third is either major (4 semitones) or minor (3), so four triads are possible.

Because the major scale contains only seven notes and two of its gaps are half steps, harmonising it yields exactly three major triads, three minor triads and one diminished triad, always in the order M m m M M m dim. The diminished triad is the only one that contains a tritone (B and F in C), which is why it is unstable and wants to resolve.

Naming: Roman numerals, figures and slash chords

Roman numerals name a chord by its scale degree: upper case for major, lower case for minor, ° for diminished, ø for half-diminished. They are key-independent, so a progression written as numbers transposes by changing one letter. Nashville notation does the same with Arabic numbers.

Inversions are named by which chord tone is lowest. In classical figured bass, a first-inversion triad (3rd in the bass) is written 6 and a second-inversion triad (5th in the bass) 6/4. In lead-sheet style they are written as slash chords: C/E is a C chord with E in the bass, C/G a C chord with G in the bass. An inversion changes the bass note and therefore the feel (first inversion is lighter and more mobile, second is unstable), but not the chord's identity.

Harmonic function

Tonal harmony is a system of tension and release around a home chord. Every diatonic chord inherits a role from the notes it shares with I, IV and V, the three primary chords.

Tonic (I, with vi and iii as substitutes) is rest. Subdominant (IV, with ii) moves away from rest. Dominant (V and vii°) is maximum tension and creates the pull back to I.

The dominant pull comes from two notes. The 7th degree is a semitone below the tonic and is called the leading tone (B to C in C). In V7 the chord's 7th (F in G7) falls a semitone to the 3rd of I (E). Together B and F form a tritone that collapses inward onto the third and root of the tonic chord. vii° is the same tritone with the root of V removed, so it behaves like a V7 without its root. iii shares two notes with both I and V, which makes it flexible and often ambiguous.

The usual order is tonic, then subdominant, then dominant, then tonic, though composers and songwriters break it constantly. The common cadences (phrase endings) are authentic (V–I, the strongest), plagal (IV–I, the "amen"), half (ending on V, an unresolved question) and deceptive (V–vi, where the ear expects I).

Keys, key signatures and the circle of fifths

Because the major scale pattern is fixed, a key signature records which notes are sharp or flat. Moving up a perfect fifth adds one sharp (the new 7th degree); moving up a fourth (down a fifth) adds one flat. Sharps are added in the order F C G D A E B, flats in the reverse order B E A D G C F.

Neighbouring keys on the circle share six of their seven notes, so modulating (changing key) to a neighbour is smooth. This is also why the vi, ii, iii and V chords of one key reappear as I, IV and others in neighbouring keys.

The relative minor of any major key starts on its 6th degree (A minor for C major) and shares the same notes and key signature. The natural minor scale is that same collection starting on vi, called Aeolian. Raising its 7th by a semitone gives the harmonic minor, which creates a major V and a leading tone and is the source of most minor-key dominant chords.

Modes and chord-scales

A mode is the same scale entered from a different starting note, with that note treated as home. The seven modes of the major scale are Ionian (major), Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian (natural minor) and Locrian. Each has a characteristic interval:

The harmonised scale and the modes are two views of one idea. Each chord of the harmonised scale is the chord that a mode's first, third, fifth and seventh notes form, so each chord implies its mode as a melodic source. Dm7 in C implies D Dorian, G7 implies G Mixolydian, and so on. This is the chord-scale relationship. It only describes available notes; what a mode actually sounds like depends on the chords underneath it, which must emphasise its characteristic note.

Extensions, alterations and tensions

Continue stacking thirds past the 7th and you reach the 9th (2nd), 11th (4th) and 13th (6th), the same notes an octave higher. These are called extensions, and chords can include them without every intermediate note. A chord that contains a note a semitone from a chord tone (such as natural 11 against a major 3rd) is dissonant; those are called avoid notes, and they explain why Lydian's #4 sounds better than a natural 4th over a major chord.

Beyond the scale

Secondary dominants are V7 chords of other diatonic chords. They temporarily point to a chord other than I, tonicising it. The notation V7/ii is read as the dominant of ii. Their foreign note is a chromatic tone from outside the scale.

Modal interchange borrows chords from the parallel minor (or other modes of the same tonic). bVII, bVI, bIII and iv are the most common borrowed chords.

Tritone substitution works because V7 and the dominant a tritone away share the same two crucial notes: the 3rd of one is the 7th of the other.

Voice leading is how individual notes move from chord to chord. Smooth voice leading keeps common tones, moves other notes by a step, and resolves tensions (7ths down, leading tones up). The 3rd and 7th of each chord define its quality and function, which is why they are called guide tones.

What this means on the guitar

Standard tuning is E A D G B E: perfect fourths between all strings except G to B, which is a major third. Two consequences follow. First, any shape built wholly on strings tuned a fourth apart is moveable across those strings without distortion, so triad shapes are identical on string sets 3-4-5 and 4-5-6 but different on 1-2-3 and 2-3-4 (which cross the G–B gap). Second, because a fret is a semitone, any chord shape with no open strings can slide up the neck to change its root, and every note on the neck repeats within about 12 frets.

Three-note triads on adjacent strings have exactly three forms, one per inversion. Learn the three on each string set and you can play every diatonic triad in every key by moving them to the right fret. This is what the string-set viewer shows.

Quick glossary

Tonic: the home note and chord. Diatonic: using only notes of the key. Chromatic: using notes outside the key. Degree: a note's number in the scale. Root: the note a chord is named after. Triad: three-note chord of stacked thirds. Inversion: a chord with a note other than the root in the bass. Cadence: a chord pair that ends a phrase. Modulation: changing key. Parallel key: same tonic, different mode (C major and C minor). Relative key: same notes, different tonic (C major and A minor).
